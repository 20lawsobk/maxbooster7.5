# Organic Social Platform Algorithm Signals (2025-2026)

**Research Date:** September 11, 2026
**Depth:** Standard
**Sources Consulted:** 20

## Executive Summary

This report documents what Instagram, TikTok, YouTube, Facebook, Threads, X, LinkedIn, and Google Business Profile have actually disclosed — officially or through credible independent reporting — about how their organic, non-paid content ranking and recommendation systems work. It was commissioned after an internal audit found that Max Booster's AI content-generation "awareness" layer had never been grounded in real algorithm research: one config file offered generic content-strategy advice with no citations, and a second, live signal-detection mechanism matched news keywords to canned phrases like "TikTok FYP algorithm shift detected" with no sourcing behind the claim. Neither qualified as research. This report — and the source-cited data file it fed into the codebase — replaces both with verified, dated, tier-rated findings.

The clearest pattern across all eight platforms is architectural: none of them rank content with one formula. Instagram, for instance, runs separate ranking systems for Feed, Stories, Explore, and Reels, each tuned to different predicted actions [[1]](https://about.instagram.com/blog/announcements/instagram-ranking-explained); Facebook's Feed pipeline narrows a candidate pool through successive scoring and contextual passes before a final blended score [[9]](https://engineering.fb.com/2021/01/26/core-infra/news-feed-ranking); X's pipeline is split into distinct candidate-generation, scoring, and re-ranking stages that are now public as open-source code [[13]](https://github.com/xai-org/x-algorithm). A second pattern is what platforms consistently decline to publish: exact numeric signal weights. Every platform in this research describes signal *categories* (completion rate, dwell time, meaningful interactions) without disclosing how heavily each is weighted in production — a deliberate choice most platforms attribute to deterring gaming of the system.

A third pattern is where the evidence gets thin: platforms are far more forthcoming about ranking *categories* than about recent changes, Shorts/Reels-specific mechanics, or precise thresholds. Claims in that space — an Instagram Reels signal hierarchy, a YouTube Shorts freshness cutoff, exact reply-vs-like weighting on Threads — recur across multiple 2026 industry sources but are not confirmed in any platform's own documentation. This report is explicit about that boundary throughout: every finding is labeled as either a platform's own stated position or independently reported/observed behavior, never blended into a single undifferentiated claim.

## Background

Max Booster's content-generation pipeline conditions its AI outputs on a "platform optimization" text block injected into every generation prompt. Investigating what backed that block surfaced two mechanisms, both non-research: a static per-platform JSON file with plausible-sounding but uncited content-strategy fields (cadence, hashtag policy, CTA style), and a live awareness service that scans real-time news/RSS signals for about ten keyword patterns (e.g. "fyp", "shadowban", "watch time") and attaches a generic canned note when one matches. Neither made a verifiable claim about how any platform's ranking algorithm actually behaves.

This report's scope is deliberately narrow: organic content ranking and recommendation — the mechanics that decide what a platform shows a user who did not pay to be shown it. Paid advertising, ad auctions, and ad targeting/bidding are explicitly out of scope; Max Booster maintains a separate research and modeling path for paid-ad delivery, and mixing the two would blur a distinction the platforms themselves maintain (Google, for instance, explicitly states that organic local placement cannot be purchased, as distinct from its paid Local Services Ads product [[16]](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7091?hl=en-en)). The intended audience is internal engineering: the findings below feed directly into a structured data file (`shared/social-platform-optimization.json`) consumed by the content-generation awareness layer, so every claim is written to be both readable by an engineer and precise enough to condition an AI generation prompt without overstating its certainty.

## Key Findings

### Finding 1: Instagram and TikTok separate ranking by surface and reward completion over reach

Instagram does not have one algorithm; it has at least four. Feed, Stories, Explore, and Reels are ranked by separate systems, each predicting a different set of likely actions from a viewer's activity, the post's metadata, and the viewer's interaction history with that specific creator [[1]](https://about.instagram.com/blog/announcements/instagram-ranking-explained). Reels ranking in particular penalizes technical execution: Instagram's own documentation states that low-resolution, watermarked, muted, or heavily text-covered video sees reduced recommendation reach [[1]](https://about.instagram.com/blog/announcements/instagram-ranking-explained). Explore, meanwhile, runs a two-stage pipeline — a lightweight candidate-generation model narrows a large pool before a heavier ranking model scores what's left [[2]](https://instagram-engineering.com/powered-by-ai-instagrams-explore-recommender-system-7ca901d2a882). A hierarchy of watch time, likes-per-reach, and DM sends-per-reach as the strongest Reels signals appears repeatedly across 2026 marketing sources and is attributed to Instagram's Adam Mosseri, but no official Instagram publication in this research pass quantifies that ordering — it is reported, not confirmed [[3]](https://vidico.com/news/instagram-reels-statistics).

TikTok's official account of its For You ranking is more specific about what does *not* matter than what does. Follower count and a creator's history of high-performing videos are explicitly named as non-factors for a given video's distribution — every video is evaluated on its own, and new accounts start on a generalized popular-content feed [[4]](https://newsroom.tiktok.com/how-tiktok-recommends-videos-for-you?lang=en). What TikTok does confirm as a strong signal is full video completion, which it says outweighs weaker contextual signals such as viewer geography [[4]](https://newsroom.tiktok.com/how-tiktok-recommends-videos-for-you?lang=en). The platform also engineers against filter bubbles directly: it avoids serving consecutive videos from the same creator or built on the same sound, and treats "Not Interested" taps or hiding a creator/sound as active negative-feedback signals that suppress future recommendations from that source [[4]](https://newsroom.tiktok.com/how-tiktok-recommends-videos-for-you?lang=en). This TikTok explanation is the platform's foundational public statement but dates to 2020; two newer official TikTok Transparency Center and Creator Academy pages could not be fetched in this research pass and are noted as a gap rather than assumed unchanged.

### Finding 2: YouTube frames "watch time" as satisfaction, not raw minutes — and an academic study complicates the clean story

YouTube's own explanation is careful to separate a click from a watch: clicking signals intent, but watch time measures what actually happened after the click [[5]](https://blog.youtube/inside-youtube/on-youtubes-recommendation-system). The platform's stated higher-order concept is "valued watch time" — time weighted by satisfaction, where satisfaction itself is partly derived from direct viewer surveys rather than inferred purely from behavior [[5]](https://blog.youtube/inside-youtube/on-youtubes-recommendation-system). Likes, dislikes, and shares feed into that satisfaction model, but YouTube states their weighting is adjusted to individual viewer habits rather than applied as one fixed global weight [[5]](https://blog.youtube/inside-youtube/on-youtubes-recommendation-system). A 2025 interview with YouTube's Senior Director of Growth & Discovery adds that recommendations increasingly incorporate viewing context — time of day, device — alongside historical behavior, and that the system can resurface older ("evergreen") videos when a trend makes them newly relevant [[6]](https://www.searchenginejournal.com/how-youtubes-recommendation-system-works-in-2025/538379/).

An academic regression study offers a genuine complication worth stating plainly rather than smoothing over: in its sample, click-through rate showed a significantly stronger association with view counts than either percentage retention or total average watch time [[7]](https://www.emerald.com/ijpcc/article/19/4/573/375567/The-relative-importance-of-click-through-rates-CTR-versus-watch-time-for-YouTube-views). This is correlational evidence from an observational sample, not a disclosure of YouTube's production ranking code, and it does not directly contradict YouTube's own framing — "watch time" in YouTube's telling already means satisfaction-adjusted time, not raw minutes, so the two claims describe different things rather than opposing ones. But it is a real, citable reason to avoid stating "watch time is the YouTube ranking signal" as an uncomplicated fact. Coverage of YouTube Shorts specifically is thin: a report of Shorts older than roughly 28-30 days being deprioritized traces to analyst observation, not an official YouTube disclosure, and is treated in this report as unconfirmed [[8]](https://www.searchenginejournal.com/youtube-shorts-algorithm-may-now-favor-fresh-over-evergreen/562661/).

### Finding 3: Facebook models what you skip as carefully as what you like, and Threads' reply-weighting became a self-acknowledged problem

Facebook's Feed ranking, per Meta's own 2021 engineering writeup, runs in three passes: a lightweight pass selects roughly 500 candidates from everything available, a first-pass model scores them independently, and a contextual pass applies rules such as content-type diversity before a final list is served [[9]](https://engineering.fb.com/2021/01/26/core-infra/news-feed-ranking). Under the hood, the system predicts several outcomes at once — the probability a given viewer will like, comment on, or share a given post — and combines those predictions into one personalized, weighted score; Meta is explicit that there is no single fixed weight assigned to any one reaction type [[9]](https://engineering.fb.com/2021/01/26/core-infra/news-feed-ranking). Meta's Transparency Center adds that both explicit signals (liking, commenting, resharing) and implicit signals (time spent viewing, scrolling past without engaging) feed the model — scrolling past is itself a modeled negative signal, not simply an absence of data [[10]](https://transparency.meta.com/rn-bi/features/ranking-and-content). One consequence of this architecture is that ranking is not purely time-decayed: new replies on an older post can reintroduce it into ranking consideration [[9]](https://engineering.fb.com/2021/01/26/core-infra/news-feed-ranking). Meta reported in October 2025 that it had begun surfacing meaningfully more same-day creator Reels, though the underlying reporting describes this as a distribution change rather than a complete disclosed formula [[11]](https://buffer.com/resources/facebook-algorithm).

Threads offers the clearest example in this research of a platform confirming a mechanic and then publicly wrestling with its side effects. Meta has confirmed that reply count, post recency, and a user's existing interaction history with accounts and posts are real Threads recommendation inputs [[12]](https://www.theverge.com/2024/10/7/24264382/threads-engagement-bait-problem-mosseri-meta). Independent reporting found that, in practice, the feed appeared to reward comments and replies more than likes or reposts — and Meta itself acknowledged this had created an exploitable "engagement bait" incentive (posts explicitly designed to provoke replies rather than to say anything) that it was actively working to correct [[12]](https://www.theverge.com/2024/10/7/24264382/threads-engagement-bait-problem-mosseri-meta). That distinction matters for content guidance: reply count is a confirmed input, but "maximize replies at any cost" is a known abuse pattern the platform is suppressing, not a clean growth tactic.

### Finding 4: X's ranking pipeline is the most transparent in this research because it is open-source code, not a blog post

Every other platform in this report discloses its ranking behavior through prose — blog posts, transparency pages, interviews. X is the exception: as of 2026, its For You feed recommendation pipeline is published as open-source code [[13]](https://github.com/xai-org/x-algorithm), which makes it the single most concrete source in this research. The published pipeline sources candidates from two places — in-network posts via a component called Thunder, and out-of-network posts surfaced through Phoenix embeddings and SimClusters engagement-based clustering — before applying filters that drop duplicates, posts older than 48 hours, and content from blocked, muted, or keyword-filtered sources [[13]](https://github.com/xai-org/x-algorithm). A scoring model then predicts several families of outcomes at once (engagement, clicks, dwell/attention, author-specific actions, and negative feedback), combines them into one weighted score, and a final re-ranking stage trades raw score against similarity to already-selected posts to keep the timeline from repeating near-duplicate takes back to back [[13]](https://github.com/xai-org/x-algorithm). The published code also documents explicit boosts for author diversity, out-of-network discovery, and newer authors [[13]](https://github.com/xai-org/x-algorithm). The one caveat worth stating plainly: published code is a snapshot, and X's product team can retrain models or adjust weights without touching the public repository, so this evidence is strong for architecture and signal *categories*, not for current numeric weights.

LinkedIn's public description is more conventional — attributed statements from the LinkedIn team rather than published code — but converges on a similar theme of predicting relevance and expertise rather than raw popularity. LinkedIn has described its ranking as built around relevance, demonstrated expertise, and engagement, specifically meaningful comments from people who already engage with a given topic area, as distinct from raw comment volume [[14]](https://buffer.com/resources/linkedin-algorithm). New content reaches a creator's existing connections first, with broader distribution beyond that network depending on engagement and inferred topical expertise [[14]](https://buffer.com/resources/linkedin-algorithm). The most concrete, longest-standing confirmed LinkedIn mechanic is dwell time, tracked two ways: how long a post stays visible in-feed once at least half of it is on-screen, and how long a viewer spends on destination content after clicking through — a design explicitly meant to penalize clickbait that produces a fast click-and-bounce [[15]](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/linkedin-now-factors-dwell-time-into-content-distribution-algorithm-whic/577839). That dwell-time reporting is from 2020, and while it continues to be cited as current through 2026, this report treats its exact calibration as unverified rather than assuming eight years of stability.

### Finding 5: Google Business Profile publishes its ranking factors directly — and is explicit about what does not affect them

Of every platform in this research, Google's Business Profile documentation is the most direct about its organic local ranking factors: it names exactly three — relevance, distance, and prominence [[16]](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7091?hl=en-en), a triad independently corroborated by Moz's local-SEO analysis [[17]](https://moz.com/blog/local-algorithm-relevance-proximity-and-prominence). Relevance is how well a profile matches the search query, which Google says complete and detailed business information helps establish [[16]](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7091?hl=en-en). Distance is physical proximity between business and searcher, or an inferred location when none is given, which is why the same business can rank differently for different searchers [[16]](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7091?hl=en-en). Prominence — how well-known a business is — draws on information from across the web, links and mentions, and explicitly on review quantity and quality, meaning more and better reviews can genuinely improve local ranking [[16]](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7091?hl=en-en); independent survey-based reporting corroborates review count and score as a real local-ranking input [[18]](https://searchengineland.com/google-my-business-and-reviews-gain-in-2020-local-ranking-factors-survey-341456). Google's documentation is also unusually direct about what does not help: it states plainly that there is no way to pay for better organic local placement, keeping this system separate from paid Local Services Ads [[16]](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7091?hl=en-en).

One claim this research could not confirm, despite it being common in SEO marketing content, is that posting frequency or Business Profile "Posts" activity directly affects local ranking. No official Google source in this research states that, and 2025's confirmed Google Search changes — three core updates and one spam update — are not documented as local-pack-specific [[19]](https://searchengineland.com/google-algorithm-updates-2025-in-review-3-core-updates-and-1-spam-update-466450). This report treats the posting-frequency claim as unverified marketing advice rather than documented mechanics, and the data file built from this research reflects that distinction explicitly rather than repeating the common claim as fact.

## Analysis

Read across all eight platforms, a consistent structural logic emerges: every one of them separates *what a piece of content is* from *who is being shown it*, and ranks per-viewer rather than assigning a single global score to a post. Facebook's multitask prediction of an individual's likelihood to like, comment, or share [[9]](https://engineering.fb.com/2021/01/26/core-infra/news-feed-ranking), Instagram's per-surface, per-viewer Reels and Explore scoring [[1]](https://about.instagram.com/blog/announcements/instagram-ranking-explained), YouTube's viewer-specific satisfaction weighting [[5]](https://blog.youtube/inside-youtube/on-youtubes-recommendation-system), and X's per-user candidate blend of in-network and out-of-network posts [[13]](https://github.com/xai-org/x-algorithm) are all instances of the same underlying design choice. For a content-generation tool, this has a direct implication: there is no universal "what the algorithm wants" — the actionable guidance is about what makes content *more likely to earn the actions each platform's model predicts on*, not about satisfying a fixed formula.

A second cross-cutting pattern is the platforms' shared silence on exact weights, which is not an oversight but appears to be policy. None of the eight platforms in this research publish a numeric weighting for their production ranking signals, even the two (X and Google) that are otherwise the most transparent about categories and architecture. X's open-source pipeline shows *which* signal families exist and how they combine structurally, but not their live numeric coefficients [[13]](https://github.com/xai-org/x-algorithm); Google names its three local factors but calls the underlying scoring confidential [[16]](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7091?hl=en-en). This is worth encoding directly into any AI-facing guidance: specific numeric claims ("comments are worth 3x a like") that circulate in marketing content are, without exception in this research, unconfirmed by the platform they describe.

A third pattern worth naming explicitly is negative or implicit signals as a first-class part of ranking, not an afterthought. Facebook models scroll-past behavior as an explicit negative signal [[10]](https://transparency.meta.com/rn-bi/features/ranking-and-content); TikTok treats "Not Interested" taps and hiding a creator or sound as suppression signals [[4]](https://newsroom.tiktok.com/how-tiktok-recommends-videos-for-you?lang=en); X's scoring explicitly includes a negative-feedback action family alongside positive engagement [[13]](https://github.com/xai-org/x-algorithm). Content guidance that only optimizes for maximizing positive actions is incomplete; avoiding the behaviors that trigger negative signals (irrelevant targeting, low-quality video, repetitive posting) is treated by every platform that discloses this level of detail as equally load-bearing.

Finally, the Threads case study is a useful caution for how this research gets applied. Threads is the one platform in this set where a confirmed ranking input (reply count) and an optimization strategy built on gaming it (engagement bait) are both documented — and the platform itself has publicly disavowed the latter while confirming the former [[12]](https://www.theverge.com/2024/10/7/24264382/threads-engagement-bait-problem-mosseri-meta). That is a reminder that "the algorithm rewards X" and "you should try to maximize X at any cost" are different claims, and conflating them is exactly the failure mode this research was meant to correct.

## Limitations

This research could not confirm early engagement velocity (first-hour or first-minutes performance) as a distinct, named ranking factor for any of the eight platforms; it is widely assumed in marketing content but no platform's own documentation in this pass stated it directly, and two search passes per the Standard-tier stop rule did not surface a primary source. No platform publishes exact numeric signal weights for its production ranking model, so all "strong signal" claims in this report describe relative emphasis or explicit category membership, never a quantified formula. Coverage of Shorts/Reels-specific mechanics beyond what is stated above is thin across Instagram, TikTok, and YouTube alike — official statements address short-form video only partially, and claims that go further (specific freshness windows, specific completion-rate thresholds) are analyst inference, not disclosure. Two official sources — TikTok's Transparency Center and Creator Academy recommendation-system pages — could not be fetched in this pass and are represented here only via search snippets. Several dates could not be independently verified beyond what appeared in search results (noted per-source in research/sources.json). As with any research into actively-maintained ranking systems, these findings are a snapshot as of September 2026; platforms change these systems without always announcing it.

## Recommendations

The immediate recommendation — replacing the two uncited mechanisms in Max Booster's awareness layer — has already been implemented alongside this report: `shared/social-platform-optimization.json` now carries a `algorithmSignals` array and a `sources` array per platform, each signal traceable to a specific, dated, tier-rated source, and the live keyword-triggered notes in the awareness service now reference specific, correctly-attributed mechanics (e.g., LinkedIn dwell time, YouTube's valued watch time) instead of generic "algorithm changed" phrasing. Two follow-on practices are worth adopting given what this research found. First, re-verify each platform's `researchedAt` date on a periodic cadence (annually is reasonable given how infrequently these platforms update their own public documentation) rather than treating the file as permanently current — several of the sources here (TikTok's 2020 explainer, LinkedIn's 2020 dwell-time reporting) are the platform's *most recent* public statement despite being years old, so "stale" and "outdated" are not the same thing, but a periodic check would catch it if that changes. Second, keep the unconfirmed/reported claims (the Instagram Reels signal hierarchy, YouTube's Shorts freshness window, Threads' reply-weighting specifics) flagged as such in any downstream consumer rather than letting them flatten into unqualified fact as the data gets reused — the distinction between "the platform said this" and "multiple blogs repeat this" is the entire value this research adds over the heuristic file it replaced.
